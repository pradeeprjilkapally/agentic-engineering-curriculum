# Lesson 21 · The team shape

**Where this gets you:** you'll be able to walk into any FDE engagement and immediately recognize the company shape you're working with, then adjust your cadence, conventions, and politics to fit.

## The idea

Solo, small, big. Each is a different FDE engagement. The same Forward Deployed Engineer who's effective in one shape can stall in another if they don't adjust. The win is knowing which shape you're in and shifting your approach to match.

**Solo founder or very small team (1–3 people).**
Move fast, near-zero permission overhead. Direct line to the decision-maker, who is usually one of the founders. No SOC2, no procurement, no legal review yet. The FDE acts almost like a temporary cofounder for the duration. The risks: founder burnout, single-point-of-failure on you, scope creep that nobody pushes back on. The wedge agents change: one person becomes five with discipline. Without discipline, one becomes half.

**Small company or scaleup (10–50 people).**
Some process, mostly still fast. The engineering leader is usually accessible, sometimes weekly. Real users, real revenue, real consequences if you break something. You ship into a stack that's been hardened by reality, which is harder than greenfield but more durable. The risks: priorities shift mid-engagement; the team you're embedded with isn't always who decides; the customer's roadmap collides with yours. The wedge agents change: leverage compounds. The work you ship in week 4 multiplies the work the team ships in month 4.

**Big company or enterprise (500+ people).**
Lots of process. Security review, procurement, legal review, change management. The decision-maker is several layers up from the engineers you'll actually work with. Long sales cycles, long deployment cycles, slow loops. Real distribution and real capital are the prize, which is why people put up with the rest. The risks: politics, mismatched incentives between the team you're with and the executives who pay you, the project living or dying on org dynamics you don't control. The wedge agents change: individual productivity gain is huge, but org-wide impact requires changing incentives, which is a different game.

How to adjust your approach:

- **In a solo shop:** ship every day, retro every Friday, write everything down because nobody else will. Build the conventions in Lesson 19 from day one even though it feels like overkill — it's not, you're the redundancy.
- **In a small company:** the engineering leader is your champion; make them look good. Document decisions obsessively because priorities will shift and you'll need to defend choices three months later. Use evals as your political shield: when a debate happens, point at the eval suite.
- **In a big company:** find the executive sponsor on week one. Spend more time than feels natural on documentation, security review, and procurement. Use the agentic discipline to deliver inside the process, not around it, or you will get bounced.

## Your first exercise

Pick a real FDE engagement you've seen or want to do. Identify which shape it is. Write down three things you would do differently from the other two shapes.

**You're done when** you can defend the three differences in a one-paragraph email to a customer's lead engineer.

**Practice proof:** save your shape analysis in `NOTES.md` under "team shape."

## Why this matters

Most engineers walk into every customer engagement the same way. The ones who win adjust their cadence, conventions, and politics for the shape they're actually in. That adjustment is invisible to the customer; what they notice is that everything goes smoothly. The cause is you, reading the shape right on day one.
